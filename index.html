    Approach and technology

    Careful at every step.

    How a visit works, the equipment we use, and the standards that keep you safe.

    DERMAÉ is a fictional clinic. This section describes the standards a real dermatology clinic should meet; it is not a claim about a real clinic.

    Your care, step by step

    1. 01

      Consultation

      Time set aside to talk, without rushing. Your dermatologist asks about your concerns and goals, your skin history, your general health, any medicines and allergies, and the products you use. Bring a list of your medicines and anything you’ve already tried. There is always time for your questions.

    2. 02

      Skin assessment

      A close look at your skin in good light, often with a dermatoscope, and with a Wood’s lamp when it helps. With your consent, photographs may be taken to compare progress later. Sometimes a test is suggested, such as a patch test, a swab, a blood test or a small skin sample. Each one is explained before it happens.

    3. 03

      Personalised plan

      Your dermatologist explains what they think is happening and the options that may help, including the option of simply waiting and watching. For each option you hear the likely benefits, the risks, the recovery time, how many visits may be needed and the cost. You get a written summary and time to decide.

    4. 04

      Treatment and follow-up

      Treatment is carried out by trained clinicians, with written aftercare instructions. A follow-up visit reviews how your skin is responding and adjusts the plan if needed. If anything worries you between visits, you’ll know how to reach the clinic.

    Diagnostics and equipment

    Dermatoscopy
    A dermatoscope is a handheld magnifier with its own light. It lets your dermatologist see patterns and colours beneath the skin’s surface that the eye alone can’t. It is used to look at moles, pigmentation, the scalp and hair. You may feel the lens resting lightly on your skin, and it takes only moments for each spot.
    Wood’s lamp examination
    A Wood’s lamp gives off a gentle ultraviolet light in a darkened room. Some pigment changes and some skin infections show up more clearly under it, which helps with diagnosis and with choosing a suitable treatment.
    Skin imaging
    Standardised photographs, taken with your consent, record how your skin or moles look on a given day. Comparing them over time helps show change, whether that is a mole being monitored or progress during treatment.
    Lasers: the main types
    • Pigment lasers target brown pigment, for some sun spots and pigmentation.
    • Vascular lasers target blood vessels, for some redness and visible vessels.
    • Resurfacing lasers work on texture. Non-ablative types heat the skin beneath an intact surface, with shorter recovery. Ablative types gently remove very thin layers of skin, with longer recovery.

    Not every laser suits every skin tone. Your dermatologist chooses the type and settings for your skin.

    Chemical peels: the main types
    • Superficial peels act on the outermost layer, with little recovery time.
    • Medium peels reach a little deeper, with several days to a week or more of recovery.
    • Deep peels are much more intensive, need significant recovery, and are rarely the first choice.

    The depth and solution are matched to your skin type and your goals.

    Safety and hygiene

    • Sterilisation

      Reusable instruments are cleaned and sterilised between every patient, following a recorded, checked process.

    • Single-use items

      Needles, microneedling tips, gloves and applicators are used once where appropriate, then disposed of safely.

    • Clean rooms

      Treatment rooms and equipment are cleaned between appointments, with hand hygiene before and after each patient.

    • Patch tests

      Before some peels and laser treatments, a small test area shows how your skin responds, before a full treatment.

    • Laser safety

      Everyone in the room wears eye protection suited to the laser, and lasers are used only by trained staff.

    • Informed consent

      Before any treatment, you’ll be told in plain language what it involves, its benefits, its risks and the alternatives. You can ask questions, take time to decide, and change your mind at any point.

    • Your privacy

      Your records are kept confidential and stored securely. Photographs are taken only with your consent, used only for your care, and never shared or used for marketing without your separate, written permission. You can ask for a chaperone at any appointment.

    Patient guide

    Before, during and after your visit.

    Practical information to help you prepare, know what to expect, and look after your skin afterwards.

    Before your first visit

    What to bring
    • Any previous reports, letters or test results about your skin
    • A list of the medicines you take, including supplements and anything bought without a prescription
    • A list of the skincare and make-up products you use, or photos of their labels
    • Photos of a flare-up, if your skin changes from day to day and may look different on the day
    • A referral letter or insurance details, if you have them
    • A few notes on the questions you’d like to ask
    On the day
    • Where possible, arrive without make-up on the areas you’d like checked, so your skin can be seen clearly.
    • If your concern involves your nails, please remove any nail polish.
    • Allow a little extra time when you arrive, in case there are forms to complete.

    During your visit

    The consultation
    Your dermatologist begins by listening: what concerns you, how long it has been happening, what you’ve already tried, your general health and the products you use. There is time for your questions throughout.
    The skin assessment
    Your skin is examined in good light, often with a dermatoscope (a handheld skin magnifier), and sometimes with a Wood’s lamp (a gentle ultraviolet light). You may be asked to uncover the areas of concern; you can keep your underwear on for a full skin check, and you can ask for a chaperone. With your consent, photographs may be taken to compare progress later.
    Your options
    Your dermatologist explains what they think is happening and the options that may help, including waiting and watching. If a test is suggested, such as a patch test or a small skin sample, it is explained first. You’ll have time to decide.

    After your visit

    Your plan and aftercare
    You’ll receive a written summary of your plan. If you’ve had a treatment, you’ll also receive written aftercare instructions. Follow them closely, and protect treated skin from the sun.
    Follow-up
    A follow-up visit reviews how your skin is responding and adjusts the plan if needed. If tests were taken, you’ll be told how and when you’ll receive the results.
    When to contact the clinic
    Contact the clinic if you have questions about your aftercare, or if anything worries you, such as redness, swelling or discomfort that is increasing rather than settling, blistering, or signs of infection like warmth, oozing or a fever.
    When to seek urgent care
    Seek urgent medical care, or call your local emergency number, if you have difficulty breathing, swelling of the face, lips or tongue, a rash that spreads quickly with a fever, or you feel very unwell.

    General information only, not medical advice. A dermatologist will assess your individual needs.
